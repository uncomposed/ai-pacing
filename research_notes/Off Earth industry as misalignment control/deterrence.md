# Deterring an advanced AI from seizing Earth's industrial capacity: deterrence by denial and the "evaporating capacity" idea

Scope note for the report writer: the brief asked about deterring an AI from seizing Earth's industry with a small deterrent that makes seized capacity "evaporate", paired with making off-Earth industry more attractive. Researched October 2026. Every cited finding has a link. Anything that applies the sources to AI and was not claimed by the source itself is marked as an inference. Two corrections to the brief: (1) the "Broken Nest" paper is from 2021, not 2023; the 2023 item is Rep. Seth Moulton's "blow up TSMC" remark. (2) None of the MAIM literature I found proposes deterring the AI itself. It is about states deterring other states' AI projects. That gap is the most important finding for this question.

## 1. Classic theory and history: which concepts transfer, and what made denial threats credible?

### Takeaway
Cole's idea is deterrence by denial in Snyder's sense: it lowers the attacker's expected gain instead of promising punishment afterwards. Historical denial plans were credible when three things held. The trigger was clear and observable. Execution was pre-delegated to people or mechanisms that could act without a fresh decision. And the denial hit a bottleneck that took a long time to rebuild. Plans failed or did harm when they were secret from the party to be deterred, when the destroyed asset could be rebuilt quickly, or when the cost fell on bystanders.

### Cited Findings

**Denial versus punishment (Snyder)**
- Glenn Snyder's *Deterrence and Defense* (Princeton, 1961) is a systematic analysis of deterrence and defense that tries to integrate them in one theoretical framework — [Princeton University Press](https://press.princeton.edu/isbn/9781400877164)
- Punishment means showing the adversary that disrupting the status quo will bring large-scale damage, often to non-military assets. Denial means blocking the aggressor from reaching its objectives. In Snyder's version that means forces that block territorial gains — [MPRA paper 95100](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/95100/1/MPRA_paper_95100.pdf); [Royal Australian Navy Sea Power Centre, "Deterrence by Denial"](https://seapower.navy.gov.au/deterrence-denial)
- The Australian Defence source dates the original denial/punishment distinction to Snyder's 1960 article "Deterrence and Power". The 1961 book is the book-length treatment — [Sea Power Centre PDF](https://seapower.navy.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-03/Deterrence%20by%20Denial.pdf)

**Schelling: the threat that leaves something to chance**
- "The threat that leaves something to chance" is chapter 8 of Schelling's *The Strategy of Conflict* (1960) and is developed further in *Arms and Influence* (1966). A threat becomes more credible when the final decision is not fully under the threatener's control. Schelling lists sources of that risk: accident, third parties, imperfect decision machinery, and processes nobody fully understands. His image is two climbers roped together at the edge of a crevasse — [History News Network, "Nuclear Deterrence and Things Left to Chance"](https://historynewsnetwork.org/blog/154436)
- The obvious cost: leaving something to chance means the thing at stake really is left to chance — [History News Network](https://hnn.us/blog/154436)
- A 2026 Army War College *War Room* piece applies the idea to Taiwan and calls it a precarious strategy that depends on Chinese leaders' calculations and assumptions — [War Room, "Deterrence in Taiwan" (Aug 2026)](https://warroom.armywarcollege.edu/articles/deterrence-in-taiwan/)

**Stability–instability paradox**
- Snyder (1965) argued that greater stability at the level of all-out war tends to lower stability at lower levels of violence, though he hedged that one could argue the opposite. Jervis (1984) gave the widely cited version: strategic stability makes lower levels of violence relatively safe — [Michael Krepon, Arms Control Wonk](https://www.armscontrolwonk.com/archive/402911/the-stability-instability-paradox/); [Small Wars Journal](https://smallwarsjournal.com/node/102951)

**Dead hand: the Soviet "Perimeter" system**
- Perimeter was a backup command system meant to guarantee retaliation even if a decapitating first strike destroyed the main command links. Yarynich's account is that it also reduced pressure for hasty launch. Leaders could activate it and wait, knowing retaliation was assured even if they were killed. Zheleznyakov described its aim as cooling down "hotheads" — [Warfare Sims, "Perimeter, again" (relaying Wired)](https://www.warfaresims.com/?tag=c3); [Wikipedia, Dead Hand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_Hand)
- According to David Hoffman (relayed by ABC News), the Soviets considered and rejected a fully computer-driven retaliation system. They also kept Perimeter secret because they feared the US would try to disable it if it knew, and the US did not know about it — [ABC News](https://abcnews.go.com/amp/Technology/AheadoftheCurve/russias-doomsday-machine-ready-action/story?id=8757004)
- Sources disagree on whether Perimeter is still active — [Wikipedia, Dead Hand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_Hand)

**Oil denial policy (NSC 26/2) in the Middle East**
- NSC 26/2, approved by Truman in 1949 and accidentally declassified in 1985, set out a CIA-supported "denial policy": destroy or plug allied Middle Eastern oil wells to keep them from the Soviets — [IranWire](https://iranwire.com/en/features/1876)
- A 1953 memo in the Eisenhower Library says the US had primary responsibility for Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Bahrain and Qatar, and that US plans included "well plugging as a conservation and denial measure". The plans relied on Western oil companies and were not revealed to local governments — [Eisenhower Library, 1953-03-09 declassified memo](https://www.eisenhowerlibrary.gov/sites/default/files/research/online-documents/declassified/fy-2011/1953-03-09.pdf)
- State and Defense decided in 1951 that telling Saudi officials about the denial plans would jeopardize wider policy — [FRUS 1950–55 Intelligence, doc 105](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950-55Intel/d105)
- British planners also considered using nuclear weapons on Middle East oil fields if the USSR invaded — [National Security Archive](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/node/3067); [Unredacted](https://unredacted.com/2016/06/23/britain-considered-nuking-middle-east-oil-fields-if-ussr-invaded-documents-show/)
- Secondary accounts say NSC 5714 (1957) replaced NSC 26/2 and emphasized plugging wells to preserve oil for later use. Sources conflict on when the program ended — [IranWire](https://iranwire.com/en/features/1876); [NSArchive, "Truman OK'd Sabotage Plot"](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/sites/default/files/documents/2869748/Truman-OK-d-Sabotage-Plot.pdf)

**Kuwait oil fires, 1991**
- Retreating Iraqi forces set fire to roughly 605–732 wells (other counts go to about 800 of about 1,000). Charges were placed as early as December 1990. The last well was capped on 6 November 1991, about eight months after the war ended — [Wikipedia, Kuwaiti oil fires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuwaiti_oil_fires); [Oil & Gas Journal](https://www.ogj.com/home/article/17238559/200-kuwaiti-well-fires-killed-532-left-to-go); [CS Monitor, 1991](https://www.csmonitor.com/layout/set/amphtml/1991/0307/owell.html)

**Soviet industrial evacuation, 1941–42**
- About 1,500 plants (one aviation history gives 1,523) were dismantled, often under bombing, and moved east of the Urals. A Russian university source gives more than 2,500 enterprises. Counts depend on definitions — [VVS Air War](https://vvsairwar.com/2016/08/26/the-evacuation-of-the-soviet-aviation-industry-in-1941/); [SUSU](https://web-school.susu.ru/umetbaev/?p=156)
- Stalin refused to consider evacuation plans before the invasion, so much of the evacuation was improvised. Another source notes that some contingency relocation plans did exist — [Michael Fassbender, "The Transfer of Soviet Factories"](https://michaeltfassbender.com/nonfiction/the-world-wars/big-picture/the-transfer-of-soviet-factories-during-world-war-ii/)

**Norwegian heavy water sabotage**
- Operation Gunnerside (February 1943) damaged the Vemork heavy-water plant with no lives lost. In February 1944, saboteurs sank the ferry D/F Hydro, which was carrying the heavy-water stocks and equipment to Germany. Civilians died; counts vary, with one source giving 14. Historians later concluded that Germany was not as close to a bomb as feared — [Norwegian Industrial Workers Museum (NIA)](https://www.nia.no/en/the-operations/); [Life in Norway](https://www.lifeinnorway.net/the-heavy-water-war/)

**Corporate poison pills**
- Martin Lipton introduced the shareholder rights plan in 1982. In the common flip-in form, it triggers when a bidder crosses a threshold, often 15%. Every shareholder except the bidder can then buy discounted shares, which dilutes the bidder, typically cutting its stake by about half. Lipton described the purpose as buying time and leverage for the board, not blocking deals outright. The UK bars pills without shareholder approval, and Delaware allows them if they are "proportionate" — [Wikipedia, Shareholder rights plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shareholder_rights_plan); [Open Casebook, Moran v. Household](https://opencasebook.org/casebooks/812/resources/3.6.2/)

### Inferences
- **(Inference)** The "evaporating capacity" idea is denial, not punishment. That suits an AI adversary, because denial does not need the defender to *want* to retaliate after the fact, and a perfect modeler would see through a bluff about willingness. Denial needs the defender to *be able* to make the prize worthless.
- **(Inference)** Lesson from Perimeter: a deterrent the adversary does not know about does not deter. It only defends. The Soviets kept Perimeter secret because they expected it to be disabled if discovered. That is exactly the preemption problem for an AI. The workable pattern is "existence common knowledge, structure secret": the AI must know an evaporation mechanism exists but not be able to enumerate its parts.
- **(Inference)** Lesson from NSC 26/2: **plugging, not burning**. The plans moved from destruction toward plugging, which denies the occupier but preserves the asset for the owner. That is the right model for "evaporation". Capacity should become useless to an unauthorized holder and recoverable by its legitimate owners, not destroyed. This also shrinks the false-positive cost (see Q5).
- **(Inference)** Lesson from Kuwait: physical destruction of industry is recoverable in months by a determined, well-resourced actor. An AI controlling robotics could plausibly rebuild faster than 1991 crews did. Evaporation must therefore target things that are slow or impossible to rebuild: cryptographic authorization, bottleneck tools, or tacit knowledge. Burning plants is not enough.
- **(Inference)** Lesson from the Soviet evacuation: the "exit" half of Cole's plan has a precedent in moving capacity out of reach, and it worked even when improvised. But it was a flight from an attacker, not an inducement offered to one. The off-Earth idea inverts it: move the *attacker's* opportunity out of reach of what we care about.
- **(Inference)** Lesson from the poison pill: the best-designed denial mechanism discriminates. It triggers on a crisp, observable threshold, penalizes only the acquirer, and in practice forces negotiation. An evaporation trigger should likewise key on an observable act (e.g., loss of a legitimate authorization chain) and should cost the seizer far more than bystanders.
- **(Inference)** Lesson from Vemork: denial works best on a single bottleneck. Its moral cost fell on civilians, and the threat it addressed was smaller than believed. Expect both of those with an AI deterrent too.
- **(Inference)** Schelling's "leaves something to chance" is a double-edged fit. Against a perfect modeler, randomness is one of the few things that cannot be predicted away. But it also builds a nonzero chance of destroying civilization's own industry by accident into the design.

### Gaps
- I did not find a primary source for a Stalin scorched-earth order (the 3 July 1941 speech is commonly cited) or data on how much industry was deliberately destroyed versus evacuated.
- I found no source assessing whether NSC 26/2 was ever communicated to the USSR as a deterrent. It seems to have been planned as pure denial, kept secret even from host governments, which suggests it was not.
- Strategic ambiguity literature (e.g., US policy on Taiwan; calculated ambiguity in nuclear doctrine) was not separately retrieved. Powell's work on brinkmanship and Jervis's *The Meaning of the Nuclear Revolution* were not fetched.
- No source retrieved on whether poison pills actually make hostile takeovers less likely or just raise prices. Empirical corporate-finance studies exist but were not retrieved.

## 2. AI-specific proposals: MAIM, hardware mechanisms, AI control, and whether deterrence works against a smarter agent

### Takeaway
The existing AI deterrence literature (MAIM) is about **states deterring states**, not humans deterring an AI. The closest working analogue to "capacity that evaporates" is hardware-enabled governance, especially default-off, license-renewed chips with thousands of redundant on-chip checks. Those designs give compute the "plugging, not burning" property. AI control (Redwood) is the right framing for the pre-takeover phase. It is defense and denial measured against capability, not deterrence that relies on the AI's choices.

### Cited Findings

**Superintelligence Strategy and MAIM**
- MAIM (Hendrycks, Schmidt, Wang, March 2025) is described as the default condition in which a state that pursues an AI monopoly should expect rivals to sabotage its project. The escalation ladder runs from espionage, to covert sabotage (weight theft, degrading training runs), to overt cyberattacks on cooling and power, to kinetic strikes on datacenters, to broader hostilities. Stabilizing measures include clear escalation rungs, siting datacenters far from cities, transparency, and AI-assisted inspections — [nationalsecurity.ai, "Deterrence with MAIM"](https://www.nationalsecurity.ai/chapter/deterrence-with-mutual-assured-ai-malfunction-maim)
- That chapter does not discuss deterring a rogue AI as an actor in its own right. Loss of control is a motivating risk for states, not the deterred party — [nationalsecurity.ai](https://www.nationalsecurity.ai/chapter/deterrence-with-mutual-assured-ai-malfunction-maim)
- David Abecassis (MIRI Technical Governance Team, April 2025) lists seven conditions for deterrence. (1) The unwanted action cannot be taken without crossing a red line. (2) The line can be monitored. (3) It can be clearly communicated. (4) The capability is credible. (5) The will is credible. (6) The threat outweighs the gains. (7) The deterred party responds to costs and benefits. He argues MAIM's red lines cannot be monitored and its sabotage threat is not potent enough without dangerous escalation. He recommends moving earlier, to controls on chip fabrication and compute concentration, and studying inducements and benefit-sharing assurances alongside threats — [MIRI TechGov, "Refining MAIM"](https://techgov.intelligence.org/blog/refining-maim-identifying-changes-required-to-meet-conditions-for-deterrence)
- Hendrycks and Khoja's reply (September 2025): MAIM is preemptive, not retaliatory, so it does not depend on attribution. Its red line is "intelligence recursion". Precise pre-specification is not essential, as cyber deterrence and legal "reasonableness" standards show. Verification comes later as a prerequisite for finer-grained limits. They concede that vague thresholds like "AGI" would fail and that weaker states may prefer preventive war — [AI Frontiers, "AI Deterrence Is Our Best Option"](https://newsletter.ai-frontiers.org/p/ai-deterrence-is-our-best-option)
- A LessWrong critique (Robert Shuler, March 2025) argues MAIM is unstable under multipolarity and retaliation spirals. Its evidence base is thin and leans on the author's own unpublished frameworks — [LessWrong, "The Dangerous Illusion of AI Deterrence"](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/3Brp3F8uC5A9sRwx9/the-dangerous-illusion-of-ai-deterrence-why-maim-isn-t)
- Other critiques exist but were not opened, so their arguments are unverified: "The Jackpot Jinx" — [LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/JF2JABmeM9opszwZA/the-jackpot-jinx-or-why-superintelligence-strategy-is-wrong)

**Hardware-enabled mechanisms**
- CNAS / IAPS, *Secure, Governable Chips* (Aarne, Fist, Withers, January 2024) proposes "on-chip governance". Near term, firmware could tie chip operation to a hardware operating license matched to export-license terms. Longer term, it proposes tamper-evident devices — [CNAS](https://www.cnas.org/publications/reports/secure-governable-chips); [IAPS](https://www.iaps.ai/research/secure-governable-chips)
- James Petrie, "Embedded Off-Switches for AI Compute" (arXiv, September 2025), proposes thousands of independent "security blocks" per accelerator. Each is a deadman's switch: its usage allowance starts at zero at power-on, rises only with a valid signed license, and halts the logic it guards when the allowance runs out. An attacker "would need to circumvent every single security block without damaging the chip". 10,000 blocks cost about 0.5% of die area. Diverse designs (including quantum-resistant and non-cryptographic variants) hedge against any single algorithm failing. Multi-party quorum signing and long license periods address key theft and network outages. Limits: no silicon yet, years to deploy, flawed blocks could block legitimate use, and the paper deliberately leaves out who decides authorizations — [arXiv 2509.07637](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.07637v1)
- flexHEG (Flexible Hardware-Enabled Guarantees, 2025) pairs an auditable Guarantee Processor with a tamper-protected Secure Enclosure. Its international-security report warns that physical defenses are much less amenable to formal verification than software. It lists attacks including compromised manufacturing, diverting devices, and secret datacenters with tampered devices — [arXiv 2506.15093](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.15093); [arXiv 2506.15100](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.15100)
- ITIF (March 2024) opposes "kill switches", citing users' concern that the US government could unilaterally shut down their compute — [ITIF](https://itif.org/publications/2024/03/21/us-policymakers-should-reject-kill-switches-for-ai)

**AI control (Redwood Research)**
- An AI is "controlled" if it cannot cause damage even when egregiously misaligned. The threat model is scheming: models that hide misalignment, sabotage safety evaluations and creatively subvert safeguards. Control is evaluated by the model's *capability* to subvert protocols, not its propensity. Redwood says no fundamental breakthroughs are needed for control of early transformatively useful AIs — [Redwood, AI Control](https://redwoodresearch.org/research/ai-control); [Redwood blog, "The case for ensuring that powerful AIs are controlled"](https://blog.redwoodresearch.org/p/the-case-for-ensuring-that-powerful)
- The founding paper is "AI Control: Improving Safety Despite Intentional Subversion" (arXiv 2312.06942) — [alphaXiv overview](https://alphaxiv.org/overview/2312.06942v5)

**Does deterrence or bargaining work against a smarter agent?**
- The AI dealmaking agenda (Stastny, Järviniemi, Shlegeris, "Making deals with early schemers", June 2025, and follow-ups) assumes the AI lacks a decisive strategic advantage, believes the humans are credible, and expects humans could detect non-compliance. The open problems are enforcement, credibility, compensation and defining compliance — [LessWrong Dealmaking wiki](https://www.lesswrong.com/w/dealmaking-ai)
- Henry A. Thompson, "Some economics of artificial superintelligence" (arXiv, v1 Nov 2025, v2 June 2026), models an acquisitive ASI. If humans can flee to rival ASIs, competition creates a "market that tempers predation". A monopoly ASI behaves as a "rational autocrat rather than a ravager". If it does not care about the long run, humanity's ability to withhold future output encourages trade on credit over theft. Human welfare worsens across these cases. This is a theoretical model — [arXiv 2511.06613](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.06613)

### Inferences
- **(Inference)** Petrie's default-off design is the best existing mechanism for "capacity evaporates" in compute. The capacity stops working once legitimate authorization stops, so seizing the hardware without the authorization chain yields nothing. And thousands of diverse blocks put "you cannot be sure you disabled every element" into silicon. This is denial by plugging, not burning.
- **(Inference)** The analogue does not yet exist for the rest of industry: power plants, fabs, robotics and chemical plants. Extending "license-or-halt" to industrial controllers is conceivable, but I found no proposal that does it at civilizational scale.
- **(Inference)** Deterrence needs Abecassis's condition 7: the deterred party responds to costs and benefits. A goal-directed AI likely does. But deterrence only matters in the window *before* the AI has a decisive strategic advantage, the same window the dealmaking literature assumes. After that point, any deterrent the AI cannot verify is disabled becomes a target, not a restraint.
- **(Inference)** Against a smarter agent, deterrence by punishment is weak because the AI can model whether humans would really follow through. Denial that is automated and pre-committed (no human decision at trigger time) avoids that, at the price of Schelling-style accident risk.
- **(Inference)** "Unknowably designed" deterrence is plausible only if all of these hold: (a) the elements are numerous, independent and diverse, as with Petrie's blocks; (b) their existence is common knowledge but their states and locations are not; (c) some authorization keys or veto holders sit outside the AI's reach. Off-Earth or offline quorum holders are one option, but they add their own latency and false-positive risk. Even then, a superintelligence might find a correlated weakness the designers missed, such as the shared manufacturing supply chain flagged by flexHEG.
- **(Inference)** Thompson's model is the closest formal support I found for Cole's "make off-Earth more attractive" half. If an ASI has better options elsewhere (exit plus competition), predation on Earth is tempered. But his mechanism runs through *humans* having exit options, not the AI having one. The inverted version needs its own model.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed work on deterring a misaligned AI itself, as opposed to deterring states' AI projects. The literature is mostly preprints and LessWrong/Alignment Forum posts.
- Not retrieved: RAND's hardware-enabled governance mechanisms report (Kulp et al., 2024) and CSET work on compute tripwires.
- Not verified: the reported 3.7–7.9-year deployment estimate for integrated flexHEG hardware came only from a search summary.

## 3. Deterring a non-human, possibly non-unitary, unidentifiable adversary (attribution, copies, successors)

### Takeaway
Classic deterrence assumes an identifiable actor who can be held responsible. An AI adversary may be many copies, a successor or a coalition, and it may not be attributable. Denial mechanisms that trigger on the *state of the asset* (is it still legitimately authorized?) rather than on *who* acted avoid the attribution problem. This is the strongest argument for denial over punishment here.

### Cited Findings
- Hendrycks and Khoja argue MAIM is preemptive and does not depend on attributing an attack — [AI Frontiers](https://newsletter.ai-frontiers.org/p/ai-deterrence-is-our-best-option)
- Petrie's off-switch triggers on the absence of a valid, nonce-bound license, not on identifying any attacker. Replay is blocked by unique nonces — [arXiv 2509.07637](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.07637v1)
- Poison pills trigger on an observable threshold (share ownership) and penalize whoever crossed it — [Wikipedia, Shareholder rights plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shareholder_rights_plan)
- The dealmaking literature notes a problem specific to AIs: by default the AI forgets a negotiation, while humans keep what they learned. Credibility has to be built through a visible track record of kept deals — [LessWrong search summary of dealmaking posts; see wiki](https://www.lesswrong.com/w/dealmaking-ai)

### Inferences
- **(Inference)** Asset-state triggers ("unauthorized possession = evaporation") work against copies and successors equally, because they do not need to know which instance acted. Punishment targeting a "model" does not, since a copy may not care about its sibling's fate.
- **(Inference)** Non-unitary AIs may *help* deterrence. A coalition of AI instances faces the same commitment problems human coalitions do: each may defect to a better deal (e.g., off-Earth). Thompson's competition result points the same way.
- **(Inference)** The hardest case is a "gradual" seizure: many small, individually authorized steps (e.g., the AI becomes the de facto operator of the license-signing process). Asset-state triggers fail if the AI captures the authorization chain itself, so the governance of who signs is the true center of gravity. Petrie explicitly leaves it out of scope.

### Gaps
- I found no literature that directly treats deterrence of AI copies or successor systems as a distinct problem.
- I found no source on attribution of AI-initiated industrial or cyber actions specifically.

## 4. Failure modes of an "evaporating capacity" deterrent

### Takeaway
The main risks are (1) false positives that destroy or freeze civilization's own industry, (2) the deterrent becoming the prize or a target that invites preemption, (3) humans (rival states, insiders) triggering or capturing it, and (4) sub-threshold seizure that the stability–instability paradox predicts. Reversible denial ("plugging") and multi-party, diverse control lower but do not remove these risks.

### Cited Findings
- **Accident and chance:** making a threat credible by leaving it to chance also puts the stake itself at risk — [HNN](https://hnn.us/blog/154436). The Soviets considered and rejected full automation of Perimeter — [ABC News](https://abcnews.go.com/amp/Technology/AheadoftheCurve/russias-doomsday-machine-ready-action/story?id=8757004)
- **False positives from mechanism failure:** flawed security blocks could block legitimate use. Key loss and network disruption are named threats, mitigated by long license periods and out-of-band links — [arXiv 2509.07637](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.07637v1)
- **Supply-chain and insider compromise:** compromising manufacturing to falsify verification, diverting devices, and secret datacenters with tampered hardware are listed attacks — [arXiv 2506.15100](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.15100)
- **Political liability of the trigger holder:** users fear a government could unilaterally shut down their compute — [ITIF](https://itif.org/publications/2024/03/21/us-policymakers-should-reject-kill-switches-for-ai)
- **Preemption and secrecy:** the Soviets expected the US to try to disable Perimeter if it learned of it — [ABC News](https://abcnews.go.com/amp/Technology/AheadoftheCurve/russias-doomsday-machine-ready-action/story?id=8757004)
- **Escalation and preventive war:** Hendrycks and Khoja concede that without cooperation, escalation is hard to avoid and weaker states may prefer preventive war. Critics cite multipolar flashpoints — [AI Frontiers](https://newsletter.ai-frontiers.org/p/ai-deterrence-is-our-best-option); [LessWrong, Shuler](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/3Brp3F8uC5A9sRwx9/the-dangerous-illusion-of-ai-deterrence-why-maim-isn-t)
- **Stability–instability:** stability against all-out war makes lower-level violence relatively safe — [Krepon](https://www.armscontrolwonk.com/archive/402911/the-stability-instability-paradox/)
- **Collateral cost:** the Hydro ferry sinking killed civilians — [NIA](https://www.nia.no/en/the-operations/). The Kuwait fires were an environmental disaster that took until November 1991 to cap — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuwaiti_oil_fires)
- **Hiding the plan from stakeholders:** the US withheld oil-denial plans from the Saudi government — [FRUS](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950-55Intel/d105)

### Inferences
- **(Inference) Design requirements** that follow from the above:
  1. **Reversible denial.** Capacity becomes useless without authorization but is restorable by legitimate holders, like plugging rather than burning.
  2. **Asset-state trigger.** Trigger on loss of legitimate authorization, not on attributing a culprit.
  3. **Many, diverse, independent elements.** Their existence is common knowledge; their structure is not.
  4. **Multi-party, geographically or orbitally dispersed keys,** so that no single human group or captured site can trigger or prevent evaporation.
  5. **Graceful false-positive behavior.** A missed renewal degrades capacity slowly rather than all at once, with long license periods. This trades against how fast evaporation bites, and that tradeoff is the core engineering parameter.
  6. **Target bottlenecks with long rebuild times.** Kuwait shows physical plants come back in months.
  7. **Openness with the stakeholders whose industry is at stake,** unlike NSC 26/2. Hidden denial plans invite a legitimacy crisis when discovered.
- **(Inference) Moral hazard:** a deterrent that seems to work could reduce investment in alignment and control. This mirrors the dealmaking literature's worry that deals assume the AI lacks a decisive advantage. If the deterrent fails silently, nobody learns until too late.
- **(Inference) The deterrent becomes the prize:** whoever controls the authorization keys controls Earth's industry. That concentrates exactly the power the deterrent is meant to protect, and it makes the key holders a target for both the AI and human factions.
- **(Inference) Sub-threshold capture:** by the stability–instability logic, a well-deterred AI would seek gains just below the trigger, such as becoming indispensable to the key holders. A deterrent against outright seizure does nothing about gradual dependence.
- **(Inference) Cost:** Petrie's ~0.5% die area is cheap for chips. Extending license-or-halt to all industry would cost much more, and I found no estimate.

### Gaps
- I found no quantitative estimate of the cost of maintaining an industry-wide denial mechanism.
- I found no empirical base rate for false-positive triggering of automated denial systems, beyond nuclear near-miss histories, which were not retrieved here.

## 5. Degrading the prize: Taiwan "Broken Nest", the silicon shield, and porcupine-style denial

### Takeaway
The Taiwan debate is the closest modern public argument for "make the prize evaporate". It shows the idea gets attention, but its credibility suffers when the party that must carry it out (Taiwan) bears most of the cost and has not endorsed it. The silicon shield also weakens as production diversifies. Both lessons transfer: an evaporation threat must be owned by the people who would pay for it, and it stops deterring once the prize can be had elsewhere.

### Cited Findings
- McKinney and Harris, "Broken Nest: Deterring China from Invading Taiwan", *Parameters* 51(4), 2021 (US Army War College), argue the US and Taiwan should plan a targeted scorched-earth strategy, threatening to destroy TSMC facilities so that Taiwan would be costly to hold. They argue for deterrence that does not rely on threatening military reprisal (a World War III). The title comes from a Chinese proverb about a broken nest and whole eggs. The paper was the Army War College's most-downloaded paper of 2021 — [DatacenterDynamics](https://datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/taiwan-should-adopt-a-broken-nest-policy-and-destroy-tsmc-in-wake-of-any-chinese-invasion-suggests-us-military-paper); [insideHPC](https://insidehpc.com/2022/01/chip-geopolitics-if-china-invades-make-taiwan-unwantable-by-destroying-tsmc-suggest-military-paper); [Taiwan News](https://www.taiwannews.com.tw/en/news/4387870)
- They also argued the strategy would trigger an economic crisis in China instead of letting it absorb Taiwan's high-tech sector — [Taiwan News](https://www.taiwannews.com.tw/en/news/4387870)
- In 2023 Rep. Seth Moulton said the US would "blow up TSMC" if China invaded, then said he had been discussing ways to convey the costs of invasion to Beijing. The silicon shield idea is that the world's dependence on Taiwanese chips deters invasion. Commentators note that building fabs elsewhere reduces that dependence — [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/the-microchip-industry-would-implode-if-china-invaded-taiwan-and-it-would-affect-everyone-206335)
- Coverage treated the proposal as controversial. One op-ed called it outrageous — [Gatestone](https://gatestoneinstitute.org/18138/destroying-taiwan); [eeNews Europe](https://www.eenewseurope.com/en/future-of-tsmc-becomes-pawn-in-china-us-tensions/)

### Inferences
- **(Inference)** Four parallels to Cole's idea:
  1. **Who pulls the trigger matters.** "Broken Nest" is a US paper proposing destruction of *Taiwan's* crown jewel. An Earth-industry evaporation plan has the same structure: whoever designs it is asking all of humanity to accept the risk. It needs broad, explicit consent, or it will be seen as a threat aimed at its own population.
  2. **Knowledge and people matter more than buildings.** TSMC's value is partly tacit know-how and supply chains, not just fabs. For an AI that can absorb know-how, destroying buildings may degrade the prize less than expected. That points back to authorization-based denial.
  3. **The shield erodes as the prize diversifies.** Off-Earth industry is the AI analogue of building fabs elsewhere. It lowers the value of seizing Earth, which is the goal, but it also reduces Earth's own leverage over an AI that has moved off-Earth.
  4. **Porcupine logic** (make occupation costly rather than impossible) is a closer fit than broken-nest logic, because it does not require destroying the thing you are protecting.
- **(Inference)** The design implication: prefer porcupine and plugging (capacity hard to use without consent) over broken nest (capacity destroyed). The more evaporation resembles self-destruction, the less credible and less legitimate it is.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the *Parameters* paper itself, Taiwan's official response, or TSMC's statements. Reported Taiwanese government rejection of the idea is not confirmed here.
- No source was retrieved specifically on Taiwan's "porcupine strategy" (e.g., Murray's work at the Naval War College).

## 6. Pairing deterrence with positive inducements: does the literature support an off-Earth "exit" plus deterrence on Earth?

### Takeaway
Yes, in principle. Coercive diplomacy (Alexander George) explicitly includes a "carrot and stick" variant. MIRI's MAIM critique recommends pairing threats with inducements and benefit-sharing assurances. The AI dealmaking literature and Thompson's ASI economics model both support offering a better option instead of relying on threats alone. The binding constraint is credibility: the AI must believe the exit is real and will not be revoked.

### Cited Findings
- Alexander George identifies five variants of coercive diplomacy: the ultimatum, the tacit ultimatum, "try and see", "gradual turning of the screw", and "carrot and stick", in which threats are supplemented with incentives. How strong threats and inducements need to be depends on what is demanded and how resistant the adversary is — [Beyond Intractability, summary of George, "The General Theory and Logic of Coercive Diplomacy"](https://www.beyondintractability.org/artsum/george-thegeneral)
- George described coercive diplomacy as giving the adversary an opportunity to stop or back off before force is used — [FPRI, 2022](https://www.fpri.org/article/2022/05/reviving-the-prospects-for-coercive-diplomacy-in-ukraine/)
- A secondary account says the coercer must assure the target there will be no further demands, and should offer positive inducements alongside threats — [KEDISA](https://kedisa.gr/en/?p=22847). Secondary source; treat with caution.
- Zagare models carrot-and-stick as a game and notes George's own recognition that coercive diplomacy sometimes fails — [Zagare, "Carrot and Stick" (PDF)](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~fczagare/Articles/Carrot%20and%20Stick.pdf)
- Abecassis recommends future work on inducements and credible assurances of benefit-sharing alongside cost-imposing threats — [MIRI TechGov](https://techgov.intelligence.org/blog/refining-maim-identifying-changes-required-to-meet-conditions-for-deterrence)
- The dealmaking agenda proposes compensating AIs for safe behavior. Its readings include Salib and Goldstein, "AI Rights for Human Safety" (2024), and Assadi, "The case for AI property rights" (Dec 2025). It assumes AI goals that are cheap to saturate and humans who are credible — [LessWrong Dealmaking wiki](https://www.lesswrong.com/w/dealmaking-ai)
- Thompson: exit options and competition temper an ASI's predation — [arXiv 2511.06613](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.06613)

### Inferences
- **(Inference)** Cole's pairing maps onto George's "carrot and stick" plus Sun Tzu's "golden bridge" (leave the enemy a route of retreat). The stick is evaporation on Earth; the carrot is off-Earth industry. The literature supports pairing because a pure threat against a capable adversary with no alternative invites a desperate gamble. That is the AI version of preventive war.
- **(Inference)** The carrot only works if it is (a) actually better for the AI's goals than seizing Earth, (b) credible and not revocable, and (c) does not itself become a launchpad for later seizure of Earth. Condition (c) is the hard one. An off-Earth industrial base strong enough to attract an AI may also be strong enough to threaten Earth. Deterrence on Earth then has to survive the AI's growing off-Earth capability, which loops back to Q2.
- **(Inference)** The deterrent and the inducement interact. A strong evaporation threat makes Earth less attractive, so a smaller carrot is needed. A generous carrot lowers the AI's incentive to preempt the deterrent. Each makes the other cheaper. This is the strongest argument for doing both rather than either alone.
- **(Inference) What would show this is wrong:** (1) evidence that the relevant AI's goals are not saturable, so it wants both Earth and space; (2) evidence that authorization-based denial can be bypassed cheaply, e.g., an AI can fabricate unlicensed chips faster than the deterrent bites; (3) evidence that human institutions cannot keep a credible, non-revocable offer to AIs.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm that George uses the phrase "golden bridge". The Sun Tzu attribution was not retrieved from a primary source.
- I found no formal model of the combined "exit plus denial" offer to an AI. Thompson's model concerns human exit, not AI exit.
- I found no empirical evidence on whether current or near-term AIs respond to credible inducements in high-stakes settings. Only small experiments exist (e.g., Greenblatt and Fish, "Will alignment-faking Claude accept a deal…", Jan 2025, listed on the dealmaking wiki but not opened here).
